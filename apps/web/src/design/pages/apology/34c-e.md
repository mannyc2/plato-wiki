## What’s happening {#happening}

Near the end of his defence, Socrates says some juror may be offended to remember how he himself, in a lesser case, begged the judges with many tears and brought forward his children to arouse pity,[^tears] while Socrates will do none of this, though apparently in the very greatest danger.[^danger] To such a man, Socrates would say that he too has relatives, being, as Homer has it, not born of an oak or a rock:[^oak] he has three sons, one nearly grown up and two still children,[^sons] but he will not bring any of them here to beg for acquittal.[^beg] Not from stubbornness, he says,[^stubborn] and whether he fears death or not is another matter;[^fear] but for the sake of his good name, the jurors’ and the whole city’s,[^name] and given his age and his reputation, deserved or not, he thinks it would not be right.[^age] At any rate, he says, the opinion prevails that Socrates is in some way superior to most men.[^superior]
