## What’s happening {#happening}

If men reputed to excel in wisdom or courage or any other virtue were to behave like that, Socrates says, it would be disgraceful;[^disgraceful] he has seen men of reputation on trial act as though they would be immortal if the jury did not kill them.[^immortal] Such men, he says, are a disgrace to the city,[^city] and the jury should be far readier to condemn a man who stages pitiable scenes than one who keeps quiet.[^pitiable] Reputation aside, he thinks it is not right to beg a judge for acquittal; one should inform and convince him.[^convince] A judge’s oath, he says, binds him to judge according to the laws, not to grant favours as he pleases,[^oath] and Socrates will not teach the jurors to break their oaths, for then, on trial for impiety, he would be teaching them not to believe in the gods.[^gods] But he does believe in them, he says, more than any of his accusers,[^believe] and so his defence closes: he leaves his case to the jury and to God, to decide as is best for him and for them.[^entrust]
