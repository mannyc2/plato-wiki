# Simmias

Simmias of Thebes,[^thebes] who raises the first of two objections to Socrates’ case: the soul may be a mixture of the body’s elements, and so the first thing to perish in what is called death.[^first] He gives the idea up,[^accept] and at the end he sees no way to doubt the result,[^result] though the greatness of the subject and human weakness still leave him some doubt.[^doubt]

:::passage{ref="phaedo 64a-b" from="And Simmias laughed" to="you made me laugh." lit="you made me laugh"}
Socrates’ claim that those who pursue philosophy aright study nothing but dying makes him laugh, though he hardly feels like it.
:::

:::passage{ref="phaedo 84d" from="Socrates, I will tell you the truth." to="your present misfortune." lit="hesitate to trouble you"}
He and Cebes have held back their doubts for fear of troubling Socrates.
:::

## How he argues {#moves}

He puts his doubt as a comparison with a lyre.[^same]

:::moves
1. In a well-tuned lyre, one might say, the harmony is invisible, incorporeal and divine,[^harmony] while the lyre and its strings are bodies, composite, earthy and akin to the mortal.[^lyre]
2. If someone broke the lyre, what if he maintained, by Socrates’ own argument, that the harmony could not have perished and must still exist?[^broken]
3. He fancies it has occurred to Socrates that they believe the soul is something like this: the body is strung and held together by heat, cold, moisture and dryness, and the soul is a mixture and harmony of these.[^mixture]
4. If the soul is a harmony, then when disease or other ills make the body too slack or too tight, the soul must perish, however divine,[^perish] while the body’s remains endure a long time.[^remains]
5. What, then, can they say to anyone who claims that the soul is the first thing to perish in what is called death?[^first]
:::

Socrates answers by asking about recollection: Simmias is as convinced as Cebes that learning is recollection, and would be much surprised ever to think otherwise.[^recollection] But a harmony, Socrates says, is the last of all to be composed and the first to perish,[^last] and Simmias cannot at all bring that into harmony with recollection.[^cannot] Asked to choose, he keeps recollection: the other theory came to him without demonstration and merely seemed probable and attractive,[^probable] and he cannot accept from himself or anyone else that the soul is a harmony.[^accept]

Socrates argues further that, on the harmony view, all souls of all living creatures would be equally good,[^good] which Simmias does not think true in the least.[^notleast] Last, Socrates says, the soul leads the body and opposes it, as a harmony could not,[^leads] and Simmias thinks that Homer, too, saw the soul as fit to lead and rule.[^homer]

## Also in the Phaedo {#cast}

:::people
- socrates: Smiles and calls his objection a fair one.[^fair]
- cebes: Does not agree with his objection, thinking the soul stronger and more lasting than the body.[^cebes]
- echecrates: Hearing of it from Phaedo, says the doctrine that the soul is a harmony has always had a hold on him.[^hold]
:::
