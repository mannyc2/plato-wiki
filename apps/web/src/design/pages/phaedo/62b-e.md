## What’s happening {#happening}

The doctrine taught in secret, that we men are in a kind of prison and must not set ourselves free or run away,[^prison] seems to Socrates weighty and not easy to understand.[^weighty] But this much he does believe is sound: that the gods are our guardians and we men are one of the chattels of the gods,[^chattels] and Cebes agrees.[^yes] Socrates asks whether Cebes would be angry with one of his own chattels that killed itself when he had not indicated that he wished it to die, and punish it if he could, and Cebes says certainly.[^angry] Then perhaps, Socrates says, it is not unreasonable to say that a man must not kill himself until god sends some necessity upon him, such as has now come upon Socrates.[^necessity] Cebes finds this sensible,[^sensible] but sets it against what Socrates said before, that philosophers ought to be ready and willing to die.[^ready] A wise man, Cebes says, does not think that when free he can take better care of himself than the gods do;[^care] a foolish man might perhaps think he ought to run away from his master,[^foolish] but a man of sense, Cebes says, would wish to be always with one who is better than himself.[^better] So the contrary seems natural, Cebes concludes: the wise ought to be troubled at dying and the foolish to rejoice.[^contrary]
