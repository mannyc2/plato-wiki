## What’s happening {#happening}

It would be absurd, Socrates says, for a man who had fitted himself all his life to live as nearly in a state of death as he could to be disturbed when death came to him.[^absurd] In fact, he tells Simmias, the true philosophers practice dying, and death is less terrible to them than to any other men.[^practice] They are in every way hostile to the body and desire to have the soul apart by itself;[^hostile] would it not be very foolish, Socrates asks, if they were frightened when this very thing happens?[^foolish] When human loves or wives or sons have died, he says, many men have willingly gone to the other world in the hope of seeing them there.[^loves] Shall a man who is really in love with wisdom, Socrates asks, grieve when he dies and not be glad to go there?[^nowhere] Not if he is really a philosopher, Socrates says, for he will confidently believe that he will find pure wisdom nowhere else.[^pure] Simmias agrees it would be very foolish for such a man to fear death.[^fear] Then, Socrates asks, is it not a sufficient sign, when a man is troubled because he is going to die,[^sign] that he was not a lover of wisdom but a lover of the body?[^body]
