# Cebes

Cebes asks the question that sets the day’s argument going: if a philosopher would desire to follow the dying, why is it not permitted to take one’s own life?[^permitted] Later he objects that the soul, however many births it outlasts, may perish altogether in one of its deaths,[^perish] and at the end he has nothing more to say against Socrates’ conclusion.[^nothing]

## How he argues {#moves}

Like Simmias, he puts his objection in a figure: a weaver and his cloaks.[^figure]

:::moves
1. It has been very cleverly and, if he may say so, conclusively shown, he grants, that the soul existed before it entered the body, but it does not seem to him proved that it will exist after we are dead.[^shown]
2. Against Simmias, he thinks the soul far stronger and more lasting than the body.[^superior]
3. One might say an old weaver who has died is safe somewhere, offering as proof that the cloak he wove and wore is still whole,[^weaver] since a man lasts much longer than a cloak.[^longer]
4. Anyone can see this is nonsense,[^nonsense] for the weaver wore out many cloaks and outlasted them, but perished, Cebes supposes, before the last one.[^last]
5. In the same way, one might say, each soul wears out many bodies,[^bodies] and when it perishes it must have on its last garment, which alone survives it.[^garment]
6. Even granting that souls may be born and die many times, one might not grant that the soul does not suffer by its many births and does not finally perish altogether in one of its deaths.[^perish]
7. One might say that no one knows beforehand which death destroys the soul;[^knows] if so, anyone confident about death has a foolish confidence, unless he can show that the soul is altogether immortal and imperishable.[^foolish]
:::

Of the earlier arguments, Cebes still believes the one from recollection more firmly than any other.[^firmly] When Socrates restates the objection, Cebes does not at present wish to add anything to it or take anything away.[^meaning] Answering it, Socrates says, means investigating completely the cause of generation and decay,[^cause] and Cebes grants him that beauty, the good, greatness and the like exist.[^grant]

From there Socrates argues that nothing which brings an opposite will admit the opposite of what it brings;[^brings] the soul always brings life,[^life] so it does not admit death and is immortal,[^deathless] which Cebes thinks very satisfactorily proved.[^proved] Socrates says the soul would be imperishable as well as immortal only if the immortal is conceded to be imperishable.[^conceded] Cebes concedes it, since nothing would escape destruction if the immortal, being everlasting, could perish.[^everlasting] So, Socrates concludes, when death comes the mortal part, it seems, dies, and the immortal part goes away unharmed.[^unharmed] Cebes has nothing more to say against that, and cannot doubt the conclusions.[^nothing]
